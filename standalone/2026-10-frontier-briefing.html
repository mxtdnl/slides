Your incident risk. An incident costs public trust and cuts your revenue that quarter.

Safety spend is the second of the three decisions. It is a percentage of a reference budget, from 0% to 30%, and every firm starts at 10%. It costs cash every quarter, and the estimate on your phone includes it. In return it lowers two things: your public exposure, the same meter as on the pace slide, and your incident risk. Incident risk rises with pace and falls with safety spend. When a firm has an incident, the projector names it, public trust falls and that firm loses revenue that quarter. A pact can also set a minimum safety spend. About 1.5 minutes.

Part 2 · Debrief, after the results

The tragedy of the commons

Why firms that each acted in their own interest used up the public trust every firm needed

2
Show this part only after the facilitator has stepped through the results screen. Until then, nothing anyone has seen has named the lesson. These five slides give the room a name for what just happened in the market, using the session's own terms. About 30 seconds.
Definition · Hardin (1968)

The tragedy of the commons

A resource that many users share is used up, because each user keeps the full gain from taking more while the cost is spread across everyone.

Example · Public trust

Every firm’s revenue depends on public trust. A faster pace builds your capability and share; the trust it draws down is lost to every firm.

Your firm keeps the gain. The whole market pays the cost.

Non-example · Market share

Capability splits the market between firms. When BLITZ or POACH lifts your share, rivals lose exactly the share you gain.

Rivalry moves value between firms and leaves the market the same size.

Ask first: which thing in FRONTIER did every firm depend on and no firm own? Take two or three answers, then read the definition. Public trust is the commons: market revenue tracked it, and every firm's pace drew it down. The non-example fixes the boundary. Market share is contested, but it always adds up to the whole market, so one firm's gain is a rival's loss and the market stays the same size. Draining trust shrinks the market for everyone. About 2 minutes.

Governing the commons · Ostrom (1990)

A pact holds when breaches are seen and cost something

1

Voluntary

Any member can leave a pact at any time, and LOBBY waives a fine in the quarter a breach is found.

Why pacts break under pressure

2

Monitoring

An audit checks a pact’s members and publishes the breaches it finds. Breaches no audit finds stay hidden until the results.

In FRONTIER: audits and the BREACH flag

3

Graduated sanctions

Each breach an audit finds brings a larger fine than the last. The third expels the firm from the pact.

In FRONTIER: rising fines, then expulsion

Monitoring and graduated sanctions are two of Ostrom's design principles for governing a commons. Read the first card as the problem: a pact anyone can leave, with a card that waives its fine, binds nobody when the race is close. The other two cards are what the market had to make a pact hold. Point to the PACT RECORD panel, which now shows breaches no audit found. Ask: which pacts held and which broke? Was the difference monitoring, sanctions or trust? Would disclosure of every firm's pace and safety from quarter 1 have changed your decisions? About 3 minutes.
