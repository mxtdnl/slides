Psychology: four effects, and the features built on them.

Effect 1 · Predict and reveal · 1 min

Intermittent reinforcement

Explain the effect in one breath: if you never know when the reward comes, you keep trying, because the next try might pay. Slot machines are built on this. Ask: which feature uses it? Type A, B, C or D in the chat. Tally with keys 1 to 4, press Lock predictions, then r. Answer: B, the endless feed. If many chose A, say read receipts come up later as a different effect. About 1 minute.
Effect 1 in the app · The endless feed

The endless feed pays out at random

A schematic drawing, not a screenshot of a real app. Sources: Montag, Lachmann, Herrlich and Zweig (2019), section 3.1; Amirthalingam and Khera (2024).

Step through the parts with r or the number keys. Part 1: there is no bottom, so you never reach a natural point to stop and reconsider. Part 2: most posts are ordinary, but now and then one is funny or interesting, and that unpredictable payoff keeps you scrolling. Part 3: autoplay, the same idea for video. Ask: when did you last decide to stop scrolling, rather than being interrupted? About 45 seconds.
Effect 2 · Predict and reveal · 1 min

Social reward

Explain: approval from other people is processed as a reward, in brain areas involved in reward. Ask which feature uses it, letters in the chat. Tally, lock, reveal. Answer: C, the Like button. If some chose B, read receipts, separate approval (a reward) from expectation (pressure, effect 4). About 1 minute.
Effect 2 in the app · The Like button

Every Like is a small social reward

A schematic drawing. Sources: Montag et al. (2019), section 3.5, citing Sherman et al. (2016); Amirthalingam and Khera (2024).

Step through with r. Part 1: one tap gives another person positive feedback; the button was co-developed by Justin Rosenstein, a former Facebook worker. Part 2: in a study of adolescents by Sherman and colleagues, photos shown with many Likes produced stronger activity in the ventral striatum, a brain area involved in processing rewards. Part 3: notifications about new Likes create urgency that pulls you back. About 45 seconds.
Effect 3 · Predict and reveal · 1 min

Social comparison

Explain: we judge ourselves against other people, and when the comparison goes badly, self-esteem can fall. Ask which feature uses it. Tally, lock, reveal. Answer: A, a feed of other people's best photos. Stress the word curated: you see their highlights and your own ordinary day. About 1 minute.
Effect 3 in the app · The curated feed

Best moments invite unfair comparison

A schematic drawing. Sources: Amirthalingam and Khera (2024), citing Chou and Edge (2012); Montag et al. (2019); Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health (2026).

Step through with r. Part 1: carefully chosen photos and idealised lives make comparison easy and rarely fair. Part 2: Likes, comments and follower numbers show how others are perceived, so you can rank yourself against them. Part 3: cosmetic filters change how faces look; the 2026 settlement bans some features associated with mental health harms, including cosmetic filters. About 45 seconds.
Effect 4 · Predict and reveal · 1 min

Social pressure and defaults

Two ideas together. Social pressure: we try to meet what others expect, such as a quick reply. Defaults: most people never change a setting, which is why the starting setting matters so much. Ask which feature uses both. Tally, lock, reveal. Answer: D, read receipts switched on by default. About 1 minute.
Effect 4 in the app · Read receipts

Two ticks turn a message into an obligation

A schematic drawing; on the real app the read ticks turn blue. Source: Montag et al. (2019), section 3.3.

Step through with r. Part 1: two grey ticks mean the message reached the other phone. Part 2: the ticks change colour when it is read; both people know the rule, so both expect a fast reply, which can undermine well-being and feed the fear of missing out. Part 3: the function comes switched on, and most users do not know they can turn it off. One anecdotal report from Microsoft found only 5% of Word users change default settings. About 45 seconds.

The Meta settlement, why regulation lags, and a vote.

If attention is this valuable, the platforms have little reason to stop on their own. So the last part is politics: what the courts did, why regulation is slow, and what you think should happen. About 15 seconds.
Politics · The case

Meta settled; the algorithms stay

FiledOctober 2023, in a US federal court
Brought byAttorneys general of 51 US jurisdictions
SettledAugust 2026, a week into the trial
ValueAbout $12 billion over 10 years; up to $17 billion

The claims

The states claimed Meta built Facebook and Instagram to keep young people hooked, and misled the public about the risks. Meta admitted no wrongdoing.

What changes

  • A two-hour daily limit
  • A block on overnight use
  • No notifications in school hours
  • Prompts to pause
  • No cosmetic filters

What stays

  • The engagement algorithms
  • Defaults young users can change
  • Other platforms to switch to
  • Terms that end after 10 years

Source: Thrul and Mendelson, interviewed by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, 16 September 2026.

Walk the fact strip, then the claims. The suit also said Meta broke the US Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule by collecting data from children it knew were under 13. Then the two lists. Optional extras users can opt into: a feed not driven by algorithms, and autoplay switched off. The limitation the Johns Hopkins researchers stress: the algorithms that keep young people on screen are not changing. An auditor watches for five years only, with limited authority. Ask the chat: which of the four effects does the settlement leave untouched? About 1 minute.
Politics · Why regulation lags

The evidence sits inside the platforms

1

Researchers cannot see the data

Independent scientists rarely get platform data, so no one outside can measure what each feature does.

2

Tests run out of sight

Platforms A/B test new versions of features on users, mostly unsolicited.

3

Cause is still debated

Problematic use is linked to anxiety and depression; whether social media causes them is open.

What accountability could look like

  • Regulate or ban certain design features
  • Pay a fee instead of paying with data
  • An archive of internal documents for researchers
  • Laws that hold companies to account on an ongoing basis

Sources: Montag et al. (2019), sections 4 and 5; Thrul and Mendelson, Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health (2026).

Three reasons regulation is slow. Without data, lawmakers cannot prove which feature does harm, and the platforms hold the data. Their own experiments, A/B tests, run unseen. And the causal link is still debated. On the right, four options from the sources. The archive idea comes from tobacco: settlements with the tobacco industry made internal documents public; nothing similar exists in the Meta settlement. About 1 minute.
