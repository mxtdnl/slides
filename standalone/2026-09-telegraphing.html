We will see what the word means, connect it to our own research on directiveness, and finish with what to telegraph and when. About 30 seconds.

Students cannot see where a session is going unless we show them.

The premise of this session

Let this sit for a moment. We know where a session is going: we planned it, and we know why each question matters. The student in front of us usually does not. Ask the room, rhetorically: in your last session, at what point did the student know what the session was for? Did you tell them, or did you assume they knew? About 1 minute.

Part 1

The word

What does it mean to telegraph a move?

1
Part 1: the word itself. We borrow it from three places, and each use adds something to how we can use it in coaching. About 6 minutes for this part.
Three contexts

Telegraphing shows the next move

1

Sport

A fighter draws back before striking. The opponent reads the movement and gets ready for the blow.

Unintended: it gives the move away.

2

Entertainment

A story plants a hint of what is coming, so the audience is ready when it arrives.

Deliberate: it prepares the audience.

3

Video games

An enemy winds up before it attacks. The wind-up is designed so the player can read the move and react.

Designed: it gives the player a chance to respond.

Coaching borrows the designer's sense: signal the move on purpose, so the student can respond.
Take the cards left to right. In sport, telegraphing is a flaw: a boxer who draws back before a punch gives the opponent time to react, so coaches train it out. In entertainment, writers do it on purpose so a later moment lands. In video games it is designed in: it makes obvious what the next move is, and when. That last sense is ours: we signal on purpose so the student can see what is coming and take part. About 3 minutes.
Definition · In coaching

Telegraphing

Making the direction and purpose of what you are doing visible to the student before you do it, explicitly and often.

Example

“Today we test your options against the goal you set last time, so you leave with one to try.”

It names the goal, the purpose and the move before they happen.

Non-example

“You should drop the module and take the internship.”

It gives the answer rather than the direction of travel. The student no longer does the thinking.

The definition has two halves: direction and purpose, and before you do it. Stress "explicitly and often": once at the start of the year is not enough. The non-example is the point of the slide. Telegraphing is a form of direction, but it points the way; it does not choose for the student. That boundary is what keeps it compatible with a non-directive stance. Ask: "Which of these would a student find more directive?" Both are, in different ways. About 2.5 minutes.

What our own research says about direction.

We have the word. Now to why it matters for our students in particular: the directiveness theme in Garcia et al. (2025), the study of our own coaching programme. About 30 seconds.
Garcia et al. (2025)

Emerging adults need a direction of travel

“An effective amount of direction in a coaching relationship with emerging adults may look like, rather than proscribing answers to problems, establishing the direction of travel when working with an emerging adult.”
Garcia, McCloy, Saati, Cassar and Neal (2025)

Students described a tension between a desire for autonomy and a need for guidance.

The study places effective direction between the prescriptive career adviser and the neutral, detached coach.

Telegraphing is one way to establish that direction without giving the answer.

An exploratory study: three student focus groups (12 students) and one coach focus group (4 coaches).

This is the directiveness theme from our own study. Students were grateful for non-directive coaching, but many also wanted structure and found parts of the experience confusing. The paper's proposal is a middle ground: set the direction of travel without prescribing answers. The banner is this session's argument, not the paper's claim: the paper does not use the word telegraphing. Be clear about the limits: a small, exploratory study with a convenience sample of highly engaged students. About 3 minutes.
Garcia et al. (2025)

Unclear purpose left some students frustrated

Some students “expected clear guidelines and actionable points and were sometimes frustrated by sessions that nudged them towards introspection”.

The authors suggest coaches understand “where more directive advice could be helpful to potentially overwhelmed students”.

What the coaches found helps

“clarifying what expectations are on both sides and providing more information about the aims and objectives of the coaching process can help with engagement”

That is telegraphing: say what the session is for before asking the student to do the work.
The frustration here is about purpose rather than method. Introspection is the right move; the student just could not see why it was happening. The paper reports that coaches found clarifying expectations and explaining the aims of coaching helped engagement, and its conclusion calls for explicit framing of the coaching role. Telegraphing does that framing at session level, and repeats it. Ask: when a student says a session "was not useful", how often is that a telegraphing gap? About 2.5 minutes.
Part 3

The practice

What do we telegraph, and when?

3
Part 3 turns the idea into something to use in the next session: three things to telegraph, four moments to do it, and a before and after. About 9 minutes for this part.
What to telegraph

Telegraph three things

1

The goal

Where we are going. Name the goal this session serves, in the student's own words where you can.

“This is about the goal you set...”

2

The purpose

Why it matters to them. Link the activity to something the student cares about, and repeat the why.

“We are doing this because...”

3

The move

What happens next and why you are doing it that way, especially before a non-directive move.

“I am going to ask rather than tell, because...”

A working structure for our practice; no published framework is implied.

Three things. The goal: where this is heading, ideally in the student's own words, so it stays their goal. The purpose: why this matters to them, and say it more than once. The move: what you are about to do and why, which matters most before a non-directive move, because that is where the frustration in the study appeared. The sentence starters are prompts, not scripts. Be clear this is our own working structure rather than a published model. About 3 minutes.
When to telegraph

Telegraph at every turn in the session

1

Opening

2

Transition

3

Before a non‑directive move

4

Close

Say where the session is going and how it connects to their goal.

Name the shift: what we just did, what comes next, and why.

Explain why you are asking rather than telling, so silence reads as purposeful.

Link what happened back to the goal and say what comes next.

Explicitly and often: assume the student has not seen the plan.
Four moments. The opening and close are familiar; the two in the middle are easier to miss. Transitions: name the shift out loud. Before a non-directive move, the taller block: an open question or a silence can read as the coach withholding help unless you say why you are doing it. "Often" is the key word. Repetition can feel redundant to us, but the student is carrying a great deal else. About 2.5 minutes.
