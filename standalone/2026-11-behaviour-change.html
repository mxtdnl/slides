Welcome. Workshop 2 gave everyone goals and indicators; today asks why the planned behaviour happens or fails to happen. Introduce B = COM as our shorthand for the COM-B model of Michie, van Stralen and West (2011): behaviour on one side, capability, opportunity and motivation on the other. Say the pun once: B = COM, become. Frame the session around their own lives: by the end, everyone will have been coached by a partner through one of their own behaviours, and will have coached their partner in turn. About 1 minute.

Part 1

Behaviour is an output

Why does a behaviour happen, or fail to happen?

1
Part 1 introduces B = COM as a system: what goes in, what comes out, and how the output feeds back. About 10 minutes, ending with a check.
B=COM

Behaviour is the output of a system. To change the output, change what goes in.

After Michie, van Stralen and West (2011): capability, opportunity and motivation interact to generate behaviour.

Let the sentence sit. Most attempts at change aim straight at the behaviour: try harder, do more. This model asks what produces the behaviour in the first place. The three inputs are capability, opportunity and motivation. Ask: "When you last failed to do something you planned, which input was missing?" Take one or two answers, but do not sort them yet; that is Part 2. About 1 minute.

Step through with r. The layout follows Figure 1 of Michie et al. (2011): single-headed arrows from capability and opportunity into motivation, double-headed arrows between each source and behaviour. Stress part 4, the double heads: doing a behaviour changes the sources. Part 5 is what B = COM must not hide: the "=" is shorthand rather than arithmetic, and changing one source can amplify or cancel a change elsewhere. Ask: "Which arrow do people usually forget?" Session map · Part 2

Three sources of behaviour

Now

What exactly are capability, opportunity and motivation, and how do you tell them apart?

Part 2 defines each source, splits it into two components, and then the room sorts real-sounding barriers. About 22 minutes, with a sort in pairs and a peer instruction question.
Definitions and core questions

Each source asks its own question

C

Capability

"The individual's psychological and physical capacity to engage in the activity concerned. It includes having the necessary knowledge and skills."

Core question

What does the person need to know or be able to do to achieve the behaviour desired?

O

Opportunity

"All the factors that lie outside the individual that make the behaviour possible or prompt it."

Core question

How can the environment be modified to achieve the behaviour desired?

M

Motivation

"All those brain processes that energize and direct behaviour, not just goals and conscious decision-making."

Core question

How can the person's plans, habits and feelings be shifted to achieve the behaviour desired?

Definitions quoted from Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). Core questions are this session's working prompts.

Read each definition, then the core question beneath it. The questions are what the room will use for the rest of the session, including on their own behaviour. Point out the reach of the motivation definition: it covers habits and emotions as well as conscious choices. And opportunity lies wholly outside the person, which is why its question is about modifying the environment rather than the person. About 4 minutes.
Six components

Each source splits in two

Capability
Opportunity
Motivation
Physical capability

Physical capacity and skill to carry out the behaviour.

e.g. the stamina for a 5 km run.

Physical opportunity

What the environment affords: time, place and things.

e.g. a gym within walking distance.

Reflective motivation

Evaluations and plans: what you judge worth doing.

e.g. valuing sleep over another episode.

Psychological capability

Capacity for the thought processes needed, such as reasoning.

e.g. knowing a referencing style.

Social opportunity

What the cultural milieu affords: how people around you think.

e.g. a flatmate who gets up early.

Automatic motivation

Emotions, impulses and habits from associative learning.

e.g. the pull of the snooze button.

Source: Michie, van Stralen and West (2011). The glosses and the student examples are this session's illustrations.

Work down each column. The distinction that matters most for coaching is in the motivation column: reflective motivation is what you decide; automatic motivation is what pulls you before you decide. Many failed plans have a sound reflective side and a losing automatic side. Ask for one example of social opportunity from student life: who you live with, what your course treats as normal. About 4 minutes.

    Intervening at the right point

    Now

    Which interventions reach which source, and what happens when they are mismatched?

    Part 3 is the second key point of the session: changing a behaviour means intervening at the source that is failing, rather than pushing on the behaviour itself. About 24 minutes, with a real case, a flawed plan and a check.

    Michie et al. place these nine intervention functions in a ring around B = COM, and around them seven policy categories, such as guidelines, fiscal measures and legislation, through which organisations and governments deliver them. Allude to the policy ring only; the coaching level is the nine functions. Select a few in turn and read the stripe: education reaches capability and motivation but not opportunity; restriction reaches only opportunity; enablement reaches everything. About 5 minutes.

      Think, pair, share · 13 min

      Your turn

      THINK is individual and silent: each person names a behaviour of their own and jots first answers. PAIR is peer coaching, about three minutes each way: the coach asks the three core questions and listens, then the coachee chooses a function; the coach checks it reaches the failing source (the table from Part 3). Coaches ask rather than advise. The timer runs per phase; when it expires the next phase lights, so press t to start it again. SHARE happens on the next slide. Circulate during PAIR and listen for interventions that target motivation by default.
