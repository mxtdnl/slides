OUTBREAK · Briefing before the simulation

Run a city
through an outbreak

Five interest groups argue. One government decides. Twelve weeks, one weekly budget.

Welcome. In a few minutes the class will run one shared response to an outbreak in a simulated city. You will play in teams: five teams each speak for one interest group, and one team is the government, which makes every decision. This briefing takes about 12 minutes and stays high level on purpose. About 30 seconds.
The situation

A new disease reaches a city of 500,000. Your class runs the response for 12 weeks.

The government team is the city’s public health director. The other teams make the case for the people they represent.

The city, Meridian City, is fictional, and so is the disease. The simulation models how it spreads week by week, and how your decisions change that. Twelve weeks are simulated; in class each week takes a few minutes. Nothing is undone: once a week has run, it has run. About 1 minute.

Government Scored on: All five groups

Hears every group, sets the plan and runs each week.

The government can put every group’s score on screen at any point in the run.

Each of the first five teams speaks for one interest group and is scored on one thing. Business on the Economy Index. General Public on public satisfaction. Vulnerable on how many clinically vulnerable people die. Healthcare on how full the hospitals get at the worst point. Treasury on value for money: good results for the money spent, so spending nothing and achieving nothing scores zero. The government team makes every decision, and is scored on all five. Assign teams now if you have not already. About 1.5 minutes.
How the government is scored

The government needs every group on side

The government’s score is the geometric mean of the five group scores.

One neglected group drags it down far more than an average would. A group on zero takes the government to zero.

Even settlement
7070707070=70GOVT

Every group at 70: the government scores 70.

One group left behind
9595959510=61GOVT

The average is 78, but the government scores 61.

BusinessPublicVulnerableHealthcareTreasury
This is the rule that makes the game work. The government cannot win by pleasing four groups and ignoring the fifth. Walk through the two rows: in the first, everyone gets 70 and so does the government. In the second, four groups are delighted and one is at 10; a plain average would say 78, but the government only scores 61. The figures are worked examples of the scoring rule, not results from a real run. Interest groups: this is your leverage. If your score is falling, say so loudly. About 1.5 minutes.
The budget

The government gets $10M every week

  • 1

    Use it or lose it. Money left at the end of a week does not carry forward.

  • 2

    No overspending. A week that costs more than the budget cannot be run.

  • 3

    Events cost too. Some responses to an event are paid from that week’s budget.

The weekly budget panel

Screenshots on these slides come from a practice run of OUTBREAK at its default settings.

The budget is $10M a week on the Standard difficulty, and it resets every week. Because unspent money is lost, leaving a lot unspent is usually a missed chance rather than a saving. The panel at the bottom left of the screen shows what you have committed and what is left; the RUN WEEK button will not work if you are over. Some events offer a choice of responses, and the paid ones come out of the same week’s money, so keep a little in reserve. About 1.5 minutes.
Two kinds of decision

Invest in capacity, or restrict how people mix

Decision InvestResource Allocation RestrictPolicy Levers
For exampleHospital beds, staff, testing, vaccines from week 4Lockdown, masks, school closures, travel limits
What it doesTreats the sick and slows the spreadSlows the spread, fast
The catchSome pay off only if bought earlyHurts the economy and satisfaction

Both cost money from the same weekly budget. Public communications and financial support make restrictions easier to bear.

Every decision the government make falls into one of these two families. Do not try to learn every slider now: each one has a short description on screen. The key idea is the trade-off. Restrictions work quickly but wear down the economy and public patience, and people comply less as patience runs out. Investments are slower to bite, and some, such as permanent hospital beds and forecasting, only help if bought before you need them. Vaccination unlocks in week 4. About 2 minutes.

The debate view

Debate view puts the plan in front of the room

The same practice run, week 5, with debate view and scores switched on.

Press D to swap the controls for this view, and D again to swap back. The strip at the top lists every decision on the table and what it costs, so the whole room can see the proposal. Press S to show or hide the group scores: each group’s score so far, and the government’s. Use S at checkpoints, for example every few weeks, rather than leaving it on. Checkpoints

Press S every few weeks to show every group’s score.

Argue is the step that takes the time. Keep it short and fair: each group gets one point per week, and the government decides. If an event offers paid responses, groups can argue about those too, since the cost comes out of that week’s budget. After RUN WEEK, read the report aloud or give the room a moment to read it before CONTINUE. Twelve weeks and the run ends. About 1.5 minutes.
The end of the run

The scorecard grades every team

From the practice run. Open it with CLASS SCORECARD on the final screen.

Press r to step through the parts. Each group gets a score out of 100 and a grade, on the same bands for everyone. The government gets the geometric mean of the five. The spread is the gap between the best-served and worst-served group: a big spread means one group carried the cost. The scorecard is the starting point for the debrief: which group lost out, and was that the right call? About 1 minute.

What you can now do

  • State the goal: protect lives, the economy and satisfaction.
  • Say what your team is scored on, and how the government is scored.
  • Explain the budget: $10M a week, use it or lose it.
  • Follow a week: event, argue, decide, report.

Remember

The government needs every group on side.

Unspent money is lost at the end of each week.

Groups: argue from your score, not just your opinion.
