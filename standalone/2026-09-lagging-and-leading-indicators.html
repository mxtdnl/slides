Part 1

Two kinds of signal

What does each indicator tell you, and when does it tell you?

1
Part 1 fixes the two terms and how they relate. Keep the pace up here: definitions, a comparison, one chain, then a check with a confidence rating. Roughly 18 minutes.
Definition

Lagging indicator

A measure of an outcome that has already happened. It tells you, with certainty, whether you reached the goal, but only once the result can no longer be changed.

Example

Last month's revenue for a subscription business.

Once the figure is known, it is too late to change: it can only be learned from.

Non-example

How often users open the product this week.

Engagement can still be acted on before it shows up in revenue, so it leads.

Source: Leung, A. X. (2025) Leading vs. lagging indicators, plus examples. Amplitude.

Stress the two halves: certain, and too late. Leung's example is monthly recurring revenue: once last month's figure is in, it cannot be changed, only learned from. The non-example matters most: engagement looks like just another number, but it arrives while you can still do something. Ask: "In your own studies, what is a lagging indicator?" Expect the final module mark. About 3 minutes.
Definition

Leading indicator

A measure that predicts a future outcome while you can still act on it. It guides what you do day to day, but it carries no guarantee.

Example

A drop in app engagement in the second week of the month.

The team can investigate and fix it before users leave and revenue falls.

Non-example

Average revenue per user.

It measures money already earned, so it reports on the past: it lags.

Source: Leung (2025), Amplitude.

The trade-off is the point: a leading indicator gives you time to act, and in exchange it is uncertain. Revenue could still fall for another reason even when engagement looks strong. Ask: "If engagement is high, is next month's revenue guaranteed?" No. Leung's advice is that leading indicators should correlate strongly with the outcome you want, which becomes our second test in Part 2. About 3 minutes.

Quality

Three tests of a good leading indicator

1

You can act on it

It moves with what you do this week, so a drop tells you what to change before the result arrives.

Ask: what would I do differently if this fell?

2

It predicts the result

It should correlate strongly with your lagging indicator. If the two part company, investigate and choose a new one.

Ask: if this rises, should my result follow?

3

You can check it often

Track it continuously, week by week. Save the lagging indicator for periodic reviews.

Ask: can I see this every week?

Tests drawn from Leung (2025), Amplitude. Feedback works best when personalised and frequent, about weekly: Fry and Neff, as reported in Trexler.

These three tests condense Leung's guidance: leading metrics let you act to affect the result, should correlate strongly with the outcome, and are tracked continuously. The Fry and Neff review (via Trexler) found reminders and feedback worked best when personalised and roughly weekly; it did not test more frequent feedback. The questions at the foot of each card are what pairs will use in Part 3. About 4 minutes.
A common trap

Some leading indicators look busy but predict little

Sign-ups can mislead: many people sign up without engaging. Leung suggests counting a milestone, such as completing onboarding, instead.

When the leading indicator is on track but the lagging one does not move, investigate and choose a new leading indicator.

Source: Leung (2025), Amplitude.

The same trap in a personal goal

Intermediate goal: be in good physical shape

Weak

Stronger

Count the behaviour that drives the result, not the easiest thing to count.
Leung's example is sign-ups versus activation: a sign-up is easy to count but many people never engage. The panel translates that to a personal goal; it is an illustration, not data. Ask the room for a study example of a busy but weak indicator: hours spent in the library is a good one, because presence is not the same as focused revision. About 3 minutes.
